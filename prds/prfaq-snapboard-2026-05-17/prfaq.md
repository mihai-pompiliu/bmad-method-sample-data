# SnapBoard PRFAQ

## Press Release

### SnapBoard Brings the Best Years of Your Family Back to the Wall

**A quiet, frame-shaped device that surfaces the photos you forgot you loved, chosen with care instead of by recency.**

**Dateline:** SEATTLE, May 17, 2027.

SnapBoard, the wall-mounted memory frame for households, is available today at snapboard.com and select home retailers. SnapBoard looks like a framed art print, hangs from a picture rail, and displays a slow, curated stream of your family's photos from the past decade. It does not show your latest camera roll. It shows what mattered.

**The problem.**

Most families take thousands of photos a year and almost never look at them again. They live in cloud libraries that are easy to back up and hard to revisit. Phones surface the newest images. Smart displays scroll through everything indiscriminately. The good photos, the ones from three years ago this week, the faces of grandparents, the trip nobody has talked about since, sink to the bottom and stay there. The archive grows. The viewing shrinks.

**The solution.**

SnapBoard treats your photo library like a garden, not a feed. A gentle on-device curation model selects images by resonance: people who have not appeared lately, places that have faded, anniversaries that quietly land this week. It chooses one photo at a time, holds it for several minutes, and rotates slowly through the day. The display is an 18-inch matte LCD with an e-ink feel, tuned to ambient light so the image looks like a print at noon and a print at midnight.

**Customer quote.**

"We hung the first SnapBoard in the kitchen and within a week my kids were stopping to look at it on the way to breakfast," said Priya Mehta, an early customer in Austin. "It put up a photo of my dad holding my son the year before he passed. Nothing else does that. Our phones definitely do not do that. It feels like the house remembers things we forgot to remember."

**Product overview.**

SnapBoard ships as a single piece of furniture: a real oak or walnut frame, a recessed matte display, a thin power cable that exits the bottom of the frame, and a picture-rail hanger. There is no logo on the front. There are no LEDs. There are no notifications. SnapBoard does one thing, slowly, in the corner of a room you already live in.

**Easy to use.**

Setup is two minutes. Plug the frame in, scan the QR code that appears with your phone, and connect Apple Photos, Google Photos, iCloud Shared Albums, Dropbox, or a local SMB share. SnapBoard begins curating within the hour. The hardware is $349. An optional Memory subscription at $4 per month extends history beyond ten years, adds multi-frame household sync, and unlocks finer-grained curation controls.

**Leader quote.**

"We did not build SnapBoard to compete with phones. We built it because phones already won, and something important got lost in the win," said Dana Okafor, founder and CEO. "Your best memories are sitting in a cloud bucket nobody opens. We wanted to put them back on the wall, where families actually live."

**Privacy posture.**

Photos travel from your cloud account to your SnapBoard over an encrypted channel and are cached on the device. Nothing leaves your household account. SnapBoard does not sell data, does not run ads, and does not have a social layer.

**Availability.**

SnapBoard is available now in oak and walnut, with a smoked ash variant arriving this fall. Visit snapboard.com to order.

## FAQ: Customer Questions

### How does SnapBoard actually choose what to show?

A small on-device model scores each photo for resonance using signals like who is in it, where it was taken, how long ago, whether similar photos have appeared recently, and whether this week of the year is meaningful (birthdays, anniversaries, trips). It does not optimize for engagement. It optimizes for "you would be glad you saw this."

### Which photo sources does SnapBoard connect to?

Apple Photos, Google Photos, iCloud Shared Albums, Dropbox, and any local SMB share on your home network. You can connect more than one. Most families connect two.

### How much does it cost?

The frame is $349 in oak or walnut. There is no required subscription. An optional Memory plan at $4 per month extends archive history beyond ten years, syncs curation across multiple frames in one household, and adds controls like "more of this person" or "skip this trip."

### Is my data safe?

Photos move from your cloud account to your SnapBoard over an encrypted channel and are cached locally on the device. SnapBoard does not share photos across households, does not train shared models on your library, and does not sell data. If you cancel the Memory subscription, the device keeps working with the standard ten-year window.

### What if I do not love a photo it picks?

There is a touch zone on the lower right of the frame. Swipe down for ten seconds to dismiss the current photo. The model learns from dismissals over time. You can also tap and hold to mark a photo as a favorite, which makes similar photos more likely.

### Why not just buy a digital photo frame?

Existing digital frames show photos in upload order or shuffle, have glossy screens that look like tablets, ship with cheap plastic bezels, require a phone app for everything, and surface the most recent photos by default. SnapBoard is the opposite of each of those choices. It is furniture, not a gadget.

### Can I have more than one in the house?

Yes. A household can pair up to four frames as a set with the Memory subscription. Frames can run synced (same photo across all four) or independent (each frame curates its own stream). Many families put one in the kitchen, one in the living room, and one in a hallway.

### What happens if someone in our family dies?

SnapBoard includes a feature called Memorial Mode. You can mark a person, and SnapBoard will continue to include them in curation with gentle frequency, will not surface photos from the date of loss for a period you choose, and will never show a photo that the household has marked private. Families have asked us to handle this carefully, and we tried.

### How does SnapBoard handle children, especially kids under 13?

Faces of children under 13 are recognized and grouped only on-device. Their face data never leaves the frame. Parents can mark a child's photos as household-only, which prevents them from appearing on any frame outside the primary residence even within a multi-home account.

### How much power does it use?

Under 4 watts on average. Less than a typical LED bulb. The display dims to near-black in a dark room and brightens gradually at dawn.

### What if Apple or Google changes their photo API and I lose access?

SnapBoard caches your selected photos locally, so existing content keeps rotating even if a cloud connection breaks. We support multiple sources specifically so no single provider can take the experience down. A local SMB share works fully offline.

### What happens when the hardware eventually fails?

The frame carries a two-year warranty. After that, the display panel is user-replaceable through our repair program for $99 plus shipping. The wood frame is designed to outlive the electronics. We commit to firmware updates for seven years from purchase.

### Can guests see what is on the frame?

Yes, and that is the point. SnapBoard is for the room, not a single account. Anyone walking through can see whatever the current rotation surfaces. If you do not want certain photos to ever appear on a particular frame, mark them private in the household app and they are excluded from that frame's pool permanently.

### Does SnapBoard work without internet?

Yes, with caveats. Once your library is connected and cached, the frame keeps rotating offline indefinitely. New photos will not flow in until the connection is restored. The local SMB option works fully offline.

## FAQ: Internal and Stakeholder Questions

### What is the TAM, and what are the unit economics?

US households with a connected cloud photo library and a wall to hang things on: roughly 60 million. Conservative serviceable market at 5 percent penetration over five years: 3 million units. Hardware BOM at scale is $138, landed cost $162, gross margin 53 percent at $349. Subscription attach modeled at 28 percent in year one, climbing to 41 percent by year three. Subscription gross margin above 80 percent. Blended LTV at year three exceeds $480.

### Why build the display panel rather than buy a reference design?

We are buying the panel, not building it. We source an off-the-shelf 18-inch IPS LCD from a tier-two Taiwanese supplier and add our own matte film, ambient-light controller, and color profile. Building a custom panel would add 18 months and $40 to BOM for marginal perceived quality gain. The matte film and tuning are where the "e-ink feel" actually comes from, and that is software plus a $3 part.

### Why a dedicated device instead of a tablet app or a TV app?

Tablets are interactive by default and invite scrolling, notifications, and decay into another screen. TVs are off most of the time and look like TVs when on. The product thesis depends on the object being ambient, always-on, single-purpose, and visually indistinguishable from a framed print. A tablet app would test a different product entirely.

### Why now?

Three things converged in the last 24 months. Cloud photo libraries crossed a decade of accumulated history for typical families, so there is finally enough archive depth for resonance-based curation to feel magical. On-device ML capable of running our curation model in under 2 watts is now under $20 in silicon. And the cultural backlash against engagement-optimized feeds has produced a real willingness to pay for calm, single-purpose hardware (see e-readers, dumb phones, the Daylight Computer).

### What does success look like in year one and year three?

Year one: 40,000 units sold, NPS above 70, subscription attach above 25 percent, return rate under 4 percent. Year three: 350,000 cumulative units, multi-frame households above 20 percent of active base, churn on Memory subscription under 6 percent annual, gross revenue above $90 million blended.

### What could kill this?

Three real risks. First, Apple or Google restricting third-party photo API access (mitigated by multi-source support and local SMB, but a hard restriction would hurt onboarding). Second, the curation quality not actually feeling magical, in which case we are an expensive picture frame (mitigated by a long internal beta and a clear dismissal signal loop). Third, a fast-follower from a major consumer electronics brand at a lower price point (mitigated by the fact that the moat is the curation model and the industrial design, both of which take time to copy well).
