# Onboarding in Small Businesses: Customer Interview Synthesis

Research lead: J. Patel. Synthesis date: 2026-05-12. Interviews conducted 2026-04-18 through 2026-05-06.

## Research Overview

Fourteen semi-structured interviews with owners and operations leads at small businesses (3 to 30 employees) across five industries. Each interview ran 45 to 60 minutes, conducted remotely over video. Participants were recruited through a mix of professional network outreach and a small-business operator Slack community. No incentives offered; participation was voluntary.

Industries represented:

- Dental practice (6 people, suburban)
- Digital marketing agency (22 people, distributed)
- Food-truck chain (18 people, three trucks, one commissary)
- Law office (9 people, civil practice)
- Specialty plumbing company (14 people, commercial focus)

Roles interviewed: 9 owner-operators, 3 operations managers, 2 office managers. None of the businesses employed a dedicated HR or People Operations role.

## Research Question

Where does onboarding break for small-business operators?

We deliberately framed the question around breakage rather than improvement, to surface friction stories rather than aspirational answers.

## Methodology Note

Interviews were transcribed and coded by two researchers independently, then reconciled. Themes below required either (a) appearance in at least 6 of 14 transcripts, or (b) strong qualitative weight from a smaller cluster with corroborating language. Where a theme came from a narrower sample, we note that explicitly. Quotes are lightly edited for clarity; ellipses indicate removed filler.

## Top Themes

### Theme 1: Onboarding is paperwork, not orientation

Heard from 12 of 14 interviewees. Owners described "onboarding" as the set of documents a new hire signs in their first two days. The remaining ramp-up was treated as a separate, mostly informal process with no clear name.

> "Onboarding to me is the paperwork day. After that it's just... they're working. There's no plan, really." (Devon, owner, plumbing)

> "We have an onboarding folder. It's a Google Drive folder with the handbook and the W-4. After they sign that we're done with onboarding." (Maria, OM at 22-person agency)

> "If you'd asked me last year I would have said we onboard great. But you mean the actual learning-the-job part? That's a totally different thing and honestly it's a mess." (Renata, owner, dental)

### Theme 2: The first week is mostly invisible to the owner

Heard from 9 of 14. Owners reported that they personally were involved for the first day, sometimes the first morning, and then had limited visibility into how the new hire's first week actually went.

> "I do the welcome lunch, I introduce them around, and then I have to go run the business. I genuinely don't know what they did on Wednesday." (Sam, owner, food trucks)

> "By Friday of week one I'll ask how it's going and they always say 'good.' I have no idea if that's true." (Jin, owner, law office)

> "The first time I find out something went wrong in week one is usually week three when a client complains." (Maria, OM at agency)

### Theme 3: Tools don't reflect the way small teams actually transfer knowledge

Heard from 10 of 14. Operators described knowledge transfer as a series of hallway conversations, shared screens, and "watch me do it once" moments. They saw existing onboarding software as built for a process they don't run.

> "Every onboarding tool I've looked at assumes I have a curriculum. I don't have a curriculum. I have Carla, and Carla knows how the billing system works." (Renata, dental)

> "The real onboarding is when somebody pulls up a chair next to a senior tech and watches three jobs. No software captures that." (Devon, plumbing)

> "We tried Trainual. It was beautiful and we never opened it again. Writing the content was the whole job and nobody had time." (Priya, OM at agency)

### Theme 4: New hires don't know what they don't know

Heard from 11 of 14, and named explicitly by 4 of them as the single hardest part of ramping someone up. Operators consistently described a class of failure where a new hire didn't ask a question because they didn't realize there was a question to ask.

> "The mistakes that hurt aren't the ones where they don't know the answer. It's the ones where they didn't know they should ask." (Jin, law office)

> "I had a new server last summer who didn't know we comp desserts for regulars. Nobody told her. Why would she ask?" (Sam, food trucks)

> "It's the unknown unknowns. You can't write a checklist for those." (Renata, dental)

### Theme 5: Institutional knowledge walks out the door

Heard from 8 of 14, with the strongest signal from businesses that had recently lost a long-tenured employee. The pattern: when someone leaves, the team only discovers what that person uniquely knew over the following months, as things break.

> "When Becca left we found out she was the only one who knew how to reconcile the merchant statements. She'd been doing it for eight years. Nobody had ever watched her do it." (Maria, agency)

> "Our head tech retired in November. We are still finding things he handled that nobody documented. It's a slow-motion disaster." (Devon, plumbing)

> "I keep thinking I should have someone shadow her before she leaves, but by the time we know she's leaving it's two weeks out." (Jin, law office)

## Anti-Themes

Things we expected to hear and largely did not.

### Anti-theme 1: Compliance paperwork is no longer a pain point

We anticipated significant friction around I-9, W-4, state withholding forms, and other compliance artifacts. Across 14 interviews, only 2 mentioned this as a current problem, and both were edge cases (one was hiring across state lines for the first time). The dominant story was that off-the-shelf payroll providers had already solved this.

> "Gusto does all that. I haven't filled out an I-9 by hand in five years." (Sam, food trucks)

> "Honestly, ADP onboarding is the one part that just works." (Priya, agency)

This is a meaningful finding because the obvious entry point for an onboarding product (handle the paperwork) is already a saturated category from the operator's perspective.

### Anti-theme 2: Owners did not ask for better dashboards or analytics

We expected at least some interest in "see how onboarding is going" dashboards. Nobody raised this unprompted. When prompted, most owners said they would not look at a dashboard.

> "I don't need a chart. I need to know my new hire isn't lost on Wednesday." (Renata, dental)

## Surprise Findings

### Surprise 1: Universal distrust of generic onboarding-checklist templates

Every operator we asked (12 of 12 asked directly) said they had tried at least one templated onboarding checklist (from a podcast, a consultant, a software vendor, or a peer) and abandoned it. The reason was consistent: the templates felt written for a different company.

> "They all feel like they were written for a different company. Like a company with an HR department and a wellness committee." (Maria, agency)

> "I downloaded one from a podcast last year. The first item was 'review the org chart.' We don't have an org chart." (Jin, law office)

### Surprise 2: Trust in artifacts that previous new hires created themselves

In contrast to template distrust, several operators (6 of 14) described high trust in onboarding artifacts created by the previous new hire. The logic: someone who just went through it knows what was confusing.

> "The best thing in our shared drive is a doc Tomas wrote on his second week. It's called 'stuff I wish someone had told me.' Every new hire reads it." (Devon, plumbing)

> "I started asking new people, at the end of month one, to write up what surprised them. Those notes are gold." (Priya, agency)

This was not something we asked about directly. It emerged unprompted in multiple conversations, which we read as a strong signal.

### Surprise 3: Warm handoffs end on day two

We expected some form of buddy system at most companies. What we found: most operators (9 of 14) assigned a buddy or shadow partner for the first day, and that pairing effectively dissolved by day three. The new hire was then on their own for questions, with no designated person to ask.

> "I pair them with someone for the first day. After that they kind of float." (Sam, food trucks)

> "There's no system for 'who do you ask in week two.' Whoever's nearest." (Renata, dental)

## Opportunity Areas

Framed as opportunity spaces, not features. Each describes a job a small-business operator is trying to get done where current tools or practices fall short.

### Opportunity 1: The unknown-unknown problem

A new hire doesn't know what they don't know. There is no current mechanism to surface the questions a new hire should be asking but isn't. Operators are aware of this gap and have no solution beyond "be available," which conflicts with their actual availability.

### Opportunity 2: The institutional-memory leak

When someone leaves, their tacit knowledge goes with them, and the team only discovers what was lost over the months following their departure. No interviewed operator had a working practice for capturing this knowledge before exit, despite all of them saying they should.

### Opportunity 3: The warm-handoff gap

Most small businesses do not have a buddy system that extends past the first day or two. Week two, week three, and week four are characterized by a new hire who has questions but no designated person to ask. This is distinct from the unknown-unknown problem; it is the known-but-no-one-to-ask problem.

### Opportunity 4: Onboarding content that doesn't feel templated

There is a clear appetite for structure but a clear allergy to generic structure. The artifacts operators trusted most were authored by people inside the business, often by recent new hires. Any product in this space has to feel like it belongs to the business, not like it was bought.

### Opportunity 5: Owner visibility without owner overhead

Owners want to know whether a new hire's first week is going well. They do not want dashboards, status meetings, or formal reviews. The unmet need is lightweight signal, not reporting.

## Non-Goals (Strongly Indicated)

Areas where we heard clear signal that the work is either solved or not wanted.

- Compliance paperwork (handled by payroll incumbents)
- Onboarding analytics and dashboards (no demand)
- Long-form curriculum authoring (operators do not have time and will not do it)

## Constraints Heard Repeatedly

- No dedicated HR or People Ops role exists in any of the 14 businesses. Any solution must work without one.
- Time investment from the owner has to be small. Operators repeatedly described being pulled into running the business and unable to dedicate sustained blocks to onboarding work.
- The team transferring knowledge is small, often one or two people who hold critical context. Solutions cannot assume redundancy.

## Recommended Next Steps

1. Run a focused round of follow-up interviews (6 to 8) specifically on the institutional-memory leak. We have qualitative signal but a smaller sample than the other themes.
2. Prototype-test the "new hire writes their own onboarding doc at week four" pattern. This emerged unprompted and could be validated cheaply.
3. Probe pricing sensitivity. We deliberately did not ask about willingness to pay in this round. Several operators mentioned current spend on payroll and operations tools, which gives us a starting baseline.
4. Investigate whether knowledge capture is better served by written artifacts or recorded conversation. Some operators referenced Loom and voice memos. Others were clearly more comfortable with text. We do not yet have a confident read.

## Open Questions Carried Forward

- Is the buyer the owner, the operations lead, or both? In our sample they were sometimes the same person and sometimes not.
- What is the right tech-stack assumption? Operators mentioned Google Workspace, Microsoft 365, Slack, and various combinations. No single dominant stack.
- Does the value land at hire time, at exit time, or continuously? The institutional-memory finding suggests exit-time value, but the unknown-unknowns finding suggests hire-time value.
